// ORIGIN

How I got here

For five years I worked at the bench across mammalian cells, microbiology, fermentation and cell-free systems, and led the wet lab for EndoSense, DTU's iGEM cell-free biosensor. Biotechnology taught me how good science actually gets made at the bench, one careful experiment at a time.

What drains me is not physical work, but repetition. Running the same protocol dozens of times leaves no room to think. Between that and countless late nights fixing broken lab equipment, one thing became clear: repetitive manual work is the real bottleneck in the lab. What energises me is the design layer, conceiving the tools, fixtures and workflows that make bench work better.

So I moved into the design layer, working with Fusion360 CAD, 3D-printed parts, custom automation, and an MSc thesis on a portable precision liquid dispenser. My years at the bench are not behind me; they are my advantage, helping me build tools for the people who actually use them. Product design is the constant, the domain stays flexible, and the logic never changes: repetitive work is the bottleneck in the lab, so I automate it.

lab automation · DALSA (DTU Arena for Life Science Automation) · 2025

Automated Gel Electrophoresis

A design-led project transferring the lab's most tedious step, manual gel loading, to an Opentrons OT-2 robot while keeping the slab gel's flexibility and low cost intact.

6-month solo project · DTU Bioengineering · graded 12/12

the prototype running the full protocol at DTU without manual pipetting.

01
the problem

A standard verification step in molecular biology, still loaded by hand.

Gel electrophoresis is a staple in molecular biology, rarely the final goal but almost always the quality-control gate: verifying a PCR reaction, inspecting a digest, or sizing DNA fragments.

Loading a gel requires pipetting samples into tiny wells by hand. The process is slow, repetitive, fatiguing, and increasingly error-prone as sample counts grow. It is low-value manual labour standing between scientists and actual discovery.

This bottleneck hits hardest where resources are constrained: academic groups, start-ups, and small biotechs. Closed cartridge systems automate the process but remain rigid and costly, while manual slab gels remain affordable and adaptable. This project bridges that gap.

02
the approach

Understand the problem before designing anything.

The project followed the Double Diamond framework (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver), exploring user needs broadly before focusing on an implementation, so the design answered a real need, not a guessed one.

  • Stakeholder analysis — a Power × Interest map placing the lab workforce (researchers, PhDs, students) at the centre as primary users facing daily friction.
  • Semi-structured interviews that revealed a clear contrast: researchers running occasional gels are satisfied with manual pipetting, while daily users handling twenty to over a hundred samples find loading a real bottleneck.
  • A market survey of 19 systems — 12 general lab-automation platforms and 7 dedicated electrophoresis systems, highlighting the opportunity between manual slab gels and expensive closed cartridge instruments.

This research defined an innovation brief: automate the most tedious steps, sample handling and gel loading, while preserving the low-cost slab-gel format. The project was carried out solo at DALSA ↗, DTU's Arena for Life Science Automation.

03
the build

Five 3D-printed components and a custom protocol on the OT-2.

The initial prototype was a custom casting cassette designed in Fusion360 with 3D-printed combs. When comb spacing conflicted with standard PCR-strip geometry, I pivoted to a more reliable solution: adapting the commercial RunOne™ MultiCaster ↗ casting system directly onto the robot deck.

Five parts followed, designed in Fusion360 with interlocking features so they seat repeatably on the deck:

  • OT-2 deck adapter — the mechanical interface between MultiCaster and deck.
  • Three PCR strip racks — positions 1–48, permanently labelled.
  • Loading-buffer & ladder rack — reagents where the protocol expects them.
  • Rack holder — locating the racks precisely.

A custom Opentrons OT-2 protocol controls the run. Instead of a rigid script, it is parameterised: operators configure strip numbers, volumes, loading buffer, and molecular-weight ladder. The protocol handles split strips half a column at a time, employs air-gap dispensing to protect low volumes, and lifts tips slowly from wells to ensure samples settle cleanly.

04
the outcome

A functional prototype and its next iteration.

144 samples per run — 3 gels × 48
~10 min per gel, tested and functional
12/12 final grade

Tested and confirmed functional: up to 144 samples per run across three gels, taking about ten minutes per gel. The system preserves slab-gel flexibility while removing manual pipetting from researchers.

Next planned improvements include gathering routine operator feedback, adding magnetic or V-groove alignment for faster assembly, modifying combs with a dedicated ladder lane, supporting diverse gel sizes, automating buffer dispensing, and adding pre-run calibration routines so the robot verifies its own positioning.

software · MSc thesis tooling and AGC Biologics · 2024 — 2026

Digital Solutions for the Lab

I built three software tools for the same reason I build hardware: routine tasks should not require manual labour. One managed my thesis testing campaign, one searches scientific literature, and one is built to coordinate shift scheduling.

three tools · Python, REST APIs, relational databases · built solo

three tools with one goal: structuring data at the moment of capture.

01
the problem

The same bottleneck, one layer up.

Five years at the bench showed me what actually exhausts scientists. It is rarely physical effort, but repetitive execution: running identical protocols with no mental space left for analysis. The projects on this site were built to eliminate that friction.

Desk work shares the exact same problem: manually typing run data into spreadsheets, querying multiple databases that rank results inconsistently, or coordinating department shifts in static files that people forget to update. None of this is genuine science, yet it consumes the hours meant for research.

The solution remains identical across domains: isolate the repetitive step and delegate it to automation. These three tools apply that principle to software rather than physical mechanisms.

02
chapter one — the testing app

A test campaign that documents itself.

A Design of Experiments campaign requires running repeated measurements in a randomised sequence, so that drift cannot pass itself off as a real effect. Executing this manually is slow and prone to sequencing or logging errors, which typically remain hidden until analysis long after testing concludes.

My MSc thesis required a dedicated software tool. Pump Validator configured, executed, and analysed test campaigns, providing the validation data for my custom liquid dispenser.

  • Data Collection — experimental parameters are established prior to measurement: operator details, hardware setup, and a Design of Experiments table of target volumes, flow rates and replicates imported from a spreadsheet, automatically generating randomised run sequences and directory structures.
  • Analysis & Dashboard — processes experiment folders to evaluate pump accuracy and precision across target volume and flow-rate ranges using interactive charts.
  • Run Campaign — automated execution: the runner drives the pump through test sequences, recording data unattended.
  • Gravimetric Analysis — sensor-free validation based on mass measurements, calculating stroke volumes, calibration curves, precision and evaporation.

The dashboards are useful, but the real point is capturing structured data immediately: organised directories, clean run logs, and standardised records. Analysis becomes an automated script rather than manual transcription.

03
chapter two — the search engine

Seven databases, one ranked list.

Literature review usually requires repeating queries across multiple databases, manually filtering duplicate papers, and reformatting references into a bibliography. This mechanical sorting consumes valuable time that should be spent actually understanding the science.

To solve this, I built a Python pipeline that queries sources simultaneously, merges ranking discrepancies into a unified feed, and exports approved references directly to my LaTeX bibliography.

  • Seven scientific APIs under one interface — unifying PubMed (NCBI), Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, Scopus (Elsevier), IEEE Xplore, arXiv and bioRxiv via Europe PMC, with per-source key handling, rate-limiting, institutional IP access for Scopus, and a source skipped cleanly if it goes down.
  • Reciprocal Rank Fusion — reconciling disparate ranking models by fusing results, scoring entries based on publication recency, total citation count, and keyword relevance in titles and abstracts.
  • Citation snowballing — bi-directional citation mapping from seed papers, combining OpenAlex for highly cited classic references with Semantic Scholar for recent work, preserving insights from both.
  • A BibTeX pipeline — abstract claim summaries, deterministic keys, diff-confirmed additions, and deduplication across citation keys, DOI, and titles.

Human-in-the-loop by design: no paper enters the bibliography without review. The pipeline handles aggregation, ranking, and citation formatting, while critical evaluation of relevant literature remains mine.

04
chapter three — the shift app

The first question was whether to build it.

My team at AGC Biologics required shift management, and commercial off-the-shelf software did not match our operational workflow. The primary decision was evaluating existing platforms thoroughly before writing any code. Once existing tools proved inadequate, the clear choice was to build an internal solution.

I designed and developed the entire application end to end, on my own initiative.

  • Full infrastructure design — system architecture, relational data schemas, and user workflows.
  • Python back end, Microsoft cloud — database-driven and responsive, so it works on a phone rather than only at a desk. I hosted it on Azure App Service to test it, and I am now preparing it for Microsoft Fabric, the platform IT asked for.
  • An API between the layers — designed structured request and response pipelines linking the user interface to the back end.
  • Through corporate IT, independently — I approached the IT department directly, mapped the infrastructure that already existed, learned their approval process for new applications and took the project through it. Getting software accepted inside a regulated biopharma environment is its own piece of engineering.

Because this software is proprietary to AGC Biologics, this case study shows the decisions, the architecture and the process. No internal data or proprietary interfaces are shown, respecting the confidentiality required in biopharma.

05
the thread

Three tools, one philosophy.

These solutions share three core principles: structure data at capture rather than during post-processing, unify fragmented sources under a clean interface, and evaluate build-versus-buy decisions pragmatically before writing code.

The mindset mirrors the hardware projects featured across this portfolio: isolate repetitive manual tasks, and build reliable tools to handle them.
