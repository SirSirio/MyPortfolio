// ORIGIN

How I got here

For five years my hands were in the lab — mammalian cells, microbiology, fermentation, cell-free systems — and I led the wet lab for EndoSense, DTU's iGEM cell-free biosensor. Biotechnology is where I learned how good science actually gets made at the bench, one careful experiment at a time.

What drains me isn't physical work — it's repetition: running the same protocol dozens of times with no room to think. Between that and countless late nights fixing broken lab equipment, one thing became impossible to ignore — repetitive manual work is the real bottleneck in the lab. What energizes me is the opposite: the design layer, conceiving the tools, fixtures and workflows that make the bench work better.

So I crossed into the design layer — Fusion360 CAD, 3D-printed parts, custom automation, an MSc thesis on a portable precision liquid dispenser. My years at the bench aren't behind me; they're the edge, and I build these tools for the people who have to use them. Product design is the constant, the domain stays flexible, and the logic never changes: repetitive work is the bottleneck in the lab, so I automate it.

lab automation · DALSA (DTU Arena for Life Science Automation) · 2025

Automated Gel Electrophoresis

A design-thinking project that hands the lab's most repetitive step — loading a gel by hand — to an Opentrons OT-2 robot, without giving up the slab gel's flexibility and low cost.

6-month solo project · DTU Bioengineering · graded 12/12

the prototype running the real protocol at DTU — no hand on a pipette.

01
the problem

A verification step almost every experiment passes through — still loaded by hand.

Gel electrophoresis is one of molecular biology's most established techniques — rarely the endpoint, almost always the quality-control gate: confirming a PCR worked, checking a digest, sizing fragments off a run.

But loading one means pipetting sample after sample into a row of tiny wells, by hand — repetitive, slow, fatiguing, easy to get wrong, and worse the more samples you run. It is low-value manual work standing between a researcher and the science itself.

The friction bites hardest where budgets are tightest — academic labs, start-ups, small biotech teams. Closed cartridge systems can automate it but are expensive and rigid; manual slab gels stay cheap and flexible. This project lives in the gap between them.

02
the approach

Understand the real problem before designing anything.

The whole project ran on the Double Diamond process — Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver — exploring the problem before converging on a solution, so the design answered a real need, not a guessed one.

  • Stakeholder analysis — a Power × Interest map putting the lab workforce — researchers, PhDs, students — at the centre, as the people who feel the friction daily.
  • Semi-structured interviews that surfaced a clear split: people who run a few gels occasionally (manual is fine for them), and people running them daily at twenty to a hundred-plus samples — for whom loading is a real bottleneck.
  • A market survey of 19 systems — 12 general lab-automation platforms and 7 dedicated electrophoresis systems — pinpointing the gap between manual slab gels and expensive closed cartridge machines.

That synthesis became an innovation brief with one direction: selectively automate the most repetitive, error-prone steps — sample handling and gel loading — while keeping the slab gel's low-cost, flexible workflow intact. Carried out solo at DALSA ↗, DTU's Arena for Life Science Automation.

03
the build

Five 3D-printed parts and a custom protocol on the OT-2.

The first prototype was a fully custom gel-casting cassette designed in Fusion360 with 3D-printed combs. It hit a manufacturability wall — the comb spacing couldn't meet standard PCR-strip geometry — so it gave way to a more robust route: mounting the existing RunOne™ MultiCaster ↗ casting system directly onto the robot's deck.

Five parts followed in Fusion360, each with interlocking cubic mating features so they seat repeatably on the deck:

  • OT-2 deck adapter — the mechanical interface between MultiCaster and deck.
  • Three PCR strip racks — positions 1–48, permanently labelled.
  • Loading-buffer & ladder rack — reagents where the protocol expects them.
  • Rack holder — locating the racks precisely.

A custom Opentrons OT-2 protocol drives the run. Rather than a fixed script it is parameterised — the operator sets how many strips, what volume, whether loading buffer and a molecular-weight ladder are included. Under the hood it loads a split strip across half a column at a time, uses air-gap dispensing to protect small volumes, and lifts the tip slowly from each well so the sample settles cleanly into its lane.

04
the outcome

A working prototype — and a clear next iteration.

144 samples per run — 3 gels × 48
~10 min per gel, tested and functional
12/12 final grade

Tested and confirmed working: up to 144 samples per run across three gels, about ten minutes per gel — keeping the slab gel's low cost and flexibility while lifting the repetitive pipetting off a person's hands.

As a prototype, it closes on where it goes next: operator feedback after repeated runs; magnetic or V-groove mating features for faster, more repeatable assembly; a modified comb with a dedicated ladder lane; support for multiple gel sizes; automated covering-buffer dispensing; and short calibration and dry-run checks so the robot verifies its own alignment before a run.

software · MSc thesis tooling and AGC Biologics · 2024 — 2026

Digital Solutions for the Lab

Three tools built for the reason I build the hardware — the repetitive part of the work should not need a person. One runs my thesis' test campaign, one reads the literature, one runs a department's shifts.

three tools · Python, REST APIs, relational databases · built solo

three tools, one instinct — structure the data at the moment it is captured.

01
the problem

The same bottleneck, one layer up.

Five years at the bench taught me what actually drains a scientist. Not the physical work — the repetition: running the same protocol dozens of times with no room left to think. Everything else on this site exists because of that.

The desk has a twin of it. Transcribing a run into a spreadsheet by hand. Chasing one query across a dozen databases that each index and rank differently. Running a department's shifts out of a file somebody has to remember to update. None of it is science, and all of it stands between a person and the science.

So the fix is the same fix, one layer up — find the step that repeats, and hand it to something that does not mind repeating. These three tools are that instinct applied to software instead of hardware.

02
chapter one — the testing app

A test campaign that writes itself down.

A Design of Experiments campaign means running the same measurement many times over, in an order deliberately randomised so that drift cannot pass itself off as an effect. Done by hand it is slow, easy to run out of order and easy to mis-key — and every one of those mistakes only surfaces later, in the analysis, when the run is long over.

So the thesis got its own instrument. Pump Validator sets a campaign up, runs it, and analyses it, and I use it to test the dispenser I am building.

  • Data Collection — the experiment is defined before anything is measured: operator, hardware configuration, and a Design of Experiments table of target volumes, flow rates and replicates that pastes straight in from a spreadsheet. It generates the randomised run order and lays out the data folders.
  • Analysis & Dashboard — loads an experiment folder and scores a pump on accuracy and precision across volume and flow-rate categories, with interactive charts.
  • Run Campaign — the automated route: the runner drives the pump through the campaign and logs the flow unattended.
  • Gravimetric Analysis — a sensor-free campaign measured by mass instead: per-stroke volume, calibration slopes, precision and evaporation.

The charts are not really the point. The point is that the data arrives structured at the moment it is captured — a run order, a folder layout, one record per measurement — so the analysis is a parse and not a retype.

03
chapter two — the search engine

Seven databases, one ranked list.

Literature discovery means running the same query across databases that each index differently and each rank differently, reading past the overlap between them, and then retyping whatever survives into a bibliography. It is the mechanical half of a thesis, and it eats the time meant for the reading.

So it became a pipeline. A Python toolchain queries every source at once, fuses their disagreeing rankings into a single list, and writes the papers I approve straight into the LaTeX bibliography.

  • Seven scientific APIs under one interface — PubMed (NCBI), Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, Scopus (Elsevier), IEEE Xplore, arXiv and bioRxiv via Europe PMC, each with its own authentication and key management, rate-limit handling, institutional-IP entitlement for Scopus, and graceful self-skipping when a source is down.
  • Reciprocal Rank Fusion — the sources disagree, so their ranked lists get fused rather than concatenated, scoring each paper on recency, citation count and how well its title and abstract match the query.
  • Citation snowballing — forward and backward traversal from a seed paper, deliberately fusing two indices that disagree: OpenAlex returns the most cited, Semantic Scholar the newest, and both halves survive the merge.
  • A BibTeX pipeline — claim extraction from abstracts, deterministic citation keys, append-with-diff-confirmation, and deduplication by key, by DOI and by near-duplicate title.

Human-in-the-loop by design: nothing is written to disk without explicit approval. The pipeline does the fetching, the merging and the formatting. The judgement about what belongs in the thesis stays mine.

04
chapter three — the shift app

The first decision was whether to build it at all.

My department at AGC Biologics needed shift management, and nothing off the shelf fitted the way the team actually works. So the real first question was not how to build it — it was whether to. That is a decision you make by looking at what already exists before you write anything, and here the answer came back build.

I am designing and developing it end to end, on my own initiative.

  • Full infrastructure design — the system architecture, the relational data model and the user flow.
  • Azure-hosted, Python back end — database-driven and mobile-friendly, because a shift app gets opened on a phone, not at a desk.
  • An API between the layers — I designed the request and response flow between the front end and the back end.
  • Through corporate IT, independently — I approached the IT department to map what infrastructure and services already existed internally, learned their intake and approval process for new applications, and took the project through that dialogue. Getting a solution accepted inside a regulated pharma IT landscape is its own piece of engineering.

The app lives inside AGC Biologics, so what is on this page is the shape of the work rather than the tool — the decisions, the architecture, the process. No screenshot, no internal detail. Knowing where that line sits is part of the job.

05
the thread

Three tools, one instinct.

They share three habits. Structure the data at the moment it is captured, never afterwards. Put one interface over many sources, so the mess stays on my side of it and not the user's. And decide honestly what to build and what to buy, because the wrong answer to that costs more than any of the code.

It is the same instinct as the hardware elsewhere on this site, in a different medium. Find the step that repeats, and take it off a person's hands.
